**The problem:** Describing what is wrong, in your own words, is where Indian healthcare first fails. Picture a 62-year-old in a district hospital queue. He tells the desk he has "gas" in his chest and is sweating. It goes down as acidity, and he waits three hours. The first gap is language: patients think in Bengali, Bhojpuri or Hinglish; forms and often staff use English or formal Hindi. The second is meaning: Indians describe illness as "gas", "ghabrahat", "chakkar" or "BP low hai", harmless or serious depending on what comes with them, and a form or a rushed clerk takes them literally. In a Mumbai study, misreading symptoms was the biggest single reason heart-attack patients reached hospital late. The doctor then gets about two minutes, much of it re-asking basics.

**Who has it, where and how often:** Anyone explaining their body in words a clinic doesn't use: a migrant worker in a new city, a farmer at the district hospital, a mother describing her child's fever. It happens at every visit, and on the phone at home when a family decides whether to go at all. It is hardest for older people, who speak slowly and often need a son or daughter to explain; by 2050 one in five Indians will be over 60.

**The voice-first product:** Bolo is a voice agent you talk to like a phone call, before the doctor sees you: at home, in the waiting room or beside the nurse. No screen, nothing to type. Step by step: (1) The patient says what is wrong, in any language or mix, and family can join in; Bolo keeps track of who said what: "maa ko do din se chakkar aa rahe hain". (2) Bolo asks one short question at a time, and for "chakkar" the one that tells its meanings apart: "Does the room spin, or do you feel you might fall?" (3) If it sounds dangerous, like "gas" in the chest with sweating at 62, it stops, tells them to get help now (112 at home) and flags the desk rather than letting them wait. (4) It reads back what it understood in their own language, and they correct it by speaking. (5) Staff get a translated one-screen intake with the patient's own words beside it, so the doctor's two minutes go to examining, not asking. It never diagnoses or suggests medicine. It works in a phone browser or desk tablet today, and next as a number any basic phone can dial.

**Why voice comes first:** Voice is not a feature added on top; it is the product. These phrases are spoken, not written: no form has a box for "seene mein bhaari lagta hai", and few patients can type Bengali or Hindi on a phone. Take away the voice and nothing is left, because the meaning lives in the patient's own words. And a phone call is the one thing almost every older Indian already knows how to use.
