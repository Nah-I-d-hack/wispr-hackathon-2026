**The problem:** Picture a 62-year-old in a district hospital queue. He tells the desk he has "gas" in his chest and is sweating. It goes down as acidity, and he waits three hours. Two gaps meet here. The first is language: patients think in Bengali, Bhojpuri or Hinglish, while forms, apps and often the staff work in English or formal Hindi. The second is meaning: Indians describe illness as "gas", "ghabrahat", "chakkar" or "BP low hai", each harmless or serious depending on what comes with it, and a form or a rushed clerk takes them literally. In a Mumbai study, misreading symptoms was the biggest single reason heart-attack patients reached hospital late. The doctor then gets about two minutes, half spent re-asking basics, while nurses write everything by hand.

**Who has it, where and how often:** Anyone explaining their body in words a clinic doesn't use: a migrant worker in a new city, a farmer at the district hospital, a mother describing her child's fever. It happens at every visit, in every OPD queue. It is hardest for older people, who speak slowly, forget details and often need a son or daughter to explain, and they are the fastest-growing group: about one in five Indians will be over 60 by 2050.

**The voice-first product:** Bolo (working name) is a voice agent you talk to like a phone call. It understands how Indians really describe illness, and takes the history before the doctor sees the patient: at home the night before, on the waiting-room bench, or beside the nurse. No screen, nothing to type: the patient or a family member just talks, in any language or mix. When they say "chakkar", it asks the one question that tells it apart: "Does the room spin, or do you feel you might fall?" When "gas" comes with sweating in an older person, it treats it as an emergency: it tells them to get help now and flags the desk, instead of letting them wait their turn. It keeps track of who said what, like the daughter adding "she hasn't eaten since Tuesday", and reads back what it understood in the patient's own language so they can correct it. Staff get a translated one-screen intake with the patient's own words beside it, so the two minutes go to examining, not asking. Bolo never diagnoses and never suggests medicine. It works in any phone browser or on a desk tablet today, and next as a number anyone can dial from a basic phone.

**Why voice comes first:** These phrases are spoken, not written. No form has a box for "seene mein bhaari lagta hai", and few patients can type Bengali or Hindi on a phone. The meaning lives in the patient's own words, so we capture speech, not English checkboxes. A phone call is the one interface every older Indian already knows, and it lets a 70-year-old answer at their own pace.
