**The problem:** Picture a 62-year-old in a district hospital queue. He tells the desk he has "gas" in his chest and is sweating. It goes down as acidity, and he waits three hours. Two gaps meet here. The first is language: patients think in Bengali, Bhojpuri or Hinglish, while forms, apps and often the staff work in English or formal Hindi. The second is meaning: Indians describe illness as "gas", "ghabrahat", "chakkar" or "BP low hai", each harmless or serious depending on what comes with it, and a form or a rushed clerk takes them literally. In a Mumbai study, misread symptoms were the biggest reason heart-attack patients reached hospital late. The doctor then gets about two minutes, much of it spent re-asking basics.

**Who has it, where and how often:** Anyone explaining their body in words a clinic doesn't use: a migrant worker in a new city, a farmer at the district hospital, a mother describing her child's fever. It happens at every visit, in every OPD queue. It is hardest for older people, who speak slowly, forget details and often need a son or daughter to explain, and they are the fastest-growing group: about one in five Indians will be over 60 by 2050.

**The voice-first product:** Bolo is a voice agent you talk to like a phone call. It understands how Indians describe illness and takes the history before the doctor sees the patient: at home the night before, on the waiting-room bench, or beside the nurse. No screen, nothing to type: just talk, in any language or mix. Indian visits are group conversations, so Bolo expects several voices: the patient, a daughter correcting the dates, a neighbour who saw the fall. It keeps track of who said what, asks the patient directly when only they can answer, and merges everyone's details into one history. When someone says "chakkar", it asks the one question that tells it apart: "Does the room spin, or do you feel you might fall?" When "gas" comes with sweating in an older person, it treats it as an emergency: it tells them to get help now and flags the desk, instead of letting them wait their turn. It reads back what it understood in the patient's language so they can correct it. Staff get a translated one-screen intake with each speaker's own words beside it, so the two minutes go to examining, not asking. Bolo never diagnoses or suggests medicine. Our prototype runs in a phone browser; next comes a number anyone can dial from a basic phone.

**Why voice comes first:** These phrases are spoken, not written. No form has a box for "seene mein bhaari lagta hai", and few patients can type Bengali or Hindi on a phone. The meaning lives in the patient's own words, so we capture speech, not English checkboxes. A phone call is the interface almost every older Indian already knows, and it lets the whole family speak, at their own pace.
