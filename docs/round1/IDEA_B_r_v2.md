**The problem:** Picture a 62-year-old in a district hospital queue. He tells the desk he has "gas" in his chest and is sweating. It goes down as acidity, and he waits his turn for three hours. Most of India describes illness in phrases like this: "gas", "ghabrahat", "chakkar", "BP low hai", "kamzori". Each one can mean something harmless or something serious, and a form, a rushed clerk or a generic chatbot takes them literally. In a Mumbai study, misreading symptoms was the biggest single reason heart-attack patients reached hospital late. Then the doctor gets about two minutes, half of it spent asking the basics again.

**Who has it, where and how often:** Anyone explaining their body in words a clinic doesn't use: a migrant worker at a city hospital, a farmer at the district hospital, a mother describing her child's fever. It happens at every visit, in every OPD queue. It is hardest for older people, who speak slowly, forget details and often have a son or daughter talking for them. They are also the fastest-growing group: about one in five Indians will be over 60 by 2050.

**The voice-first product:** Bolo (working name) takes the history while the patient waits, and it understands how Indians actually describe illness. The patient or a family member taps one button and talks, in any language or mix. When they say "chakkar", Bolo asks the one question that tells it apart: "Does the room spin, or do you feel you might fall?" When "gas" comes with sweating in an older person, Bolo treats it as an emergency, not indigestion: it tells them to get help now and flags the desk. Indian visits are family conversations, so Bolo keeps track of who said what, like the daughter adding "she hasn't eaten since Tuesday". Before finishing, it reads back what it understood in the patient's own language and lets them correct it. The doctor gets a one-screen English summary with the patient's own words beside it, so the two minutes go to examining, not asking. Bolo never diagnoses and never suggests medicine. It runs on any phone browser or a shared tablet at the desk, and next, as a number anyone can call from a basic phone.

**Why voice comes first:** These phrases are spoken, not written. No form has a box for "seene mein bhaari lagta hai", and few patients can type Bengali or Hindi anyway. The meaning lives in the patient's own words, so we capture speech instead of forcing people into English checkboxes. Voice also lets the family help naturally, and lets a 70-year-old answer at their own pace without anyone typing for them.
